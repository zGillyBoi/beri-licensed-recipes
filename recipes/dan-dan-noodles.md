# Dan Dan Noodles

Adapted from "Dan Dan Noodles" by ForkRecipe Kitchen, from the ForkRecipe dataset.
This adaptation is licensed under [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/).

Thin wheat noodles arrive buried under a thick, sesame-dark sauce that carries the mala numbing heat of Sichuan peppercorn alongside a crispy, caramelized pork mince that is almost more condiment than protein. Each toss of the bowl releases a wave of sesame paste, chili oil, and fermented vegetable brine that smells like a Chengdu street corner at noon.

Serves about 1 · 45 minutes · Contains: gluten, mustard, sesame, soy, wheat

## Ingredients

- 200 g fresh thin wheat noodles, or dried Chinese noodles
- ⅔ cup ground pork, 20% fat (150 g)
- 2½ tablespoons sesame paste, Chinese zhima jiang (40 g)
- 2 tablespoons chili oil with sediment, la you (30 ml)
- 1½ tablespoons light soy sauce (25 ml)
- 1 tablespoon Chinkiang black vinegar
- 2 tablespoons yibin ya cai, rinsed and chopped; Sichuan preserved mustard greens (30 g)
- 1½ teaspoons Sichuan peppercorns, toasted and ground to powder
- 1 tablespoon doubanjiang
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 1½ tablespoons neutral oil (20 ml)
- ¼ cup hot noodle cooking water, reserved (60 ml)
- 1¼ teaspoons granulated sugar
- 1 scallion, thinly sliced (20 g)

## Method

1. Heat a wok over high heat until wisps of smoke appear at the rim. Add the neutral oil and swirl to coat the surface. Add the ground pork in a single flat layer — resist the urge to stir. Let it cook undisturbed for 60–90 seconds until the bottom develops a crust and begins to release from the wok. Now break it apart and continue cooking, stirring occasionally, until the pork is cooked through and the pieces are individually caramelized and a little crispy — 4–5 minutes total. Push the pork to the sides of the wok. Add the doubanjiang to the hot center and fry it for 60 seconds, stirring, until it darkens and releases its red oil. Add the minced garlic and stir everything together for 30 seconds. Add the rinsed ya cai (or substitute preserved vegetable) and toss together with the pork and paste for 1 minute. The goal is crispy, concentrated, fragrant mince — not moist and soft. Every piece should be individually browned, almost crunchy at the edges, and deeply savory from the fermented paste. This is a topping and a seasoning, not a protein main.
2. Bring a large pot of unsalted water to a vigorous rolling boil. Cook the noodles according to their type: fresh noodles need only 60–90 seconds; dried thin wheat noodles typically take 3–4 minutes. Test constantly in the last 30 seconds by pulling a strand out and biting through it — you want it cooked through but still with a very slight resistance at the center, slightly firmer than you would eat it plain, because the sauce will continue softening the noodles as you toss them. Before draining, ladle out approximately 100 ml of the cloudy, starchy cooking water and set it aside — this is the binder that will loosen your sauce to the right coating consistency. Drain the noodles quickly, shaking the colander hard to remove as much water as possible. Do not rinse. A rinsed noodle refuses to hold sauce.
3. In each serving bowl, combine the sesame paste, chili oil (with its red sediment), soy sauce, black vinegar, Sichuan pepper powder, and sugar. Add 2–3 tablespoons of hot noodle cooking water and whisk with a fork or chopstick until the sauce is smooth, homogeneous, and pourable — the sesame paste tends to seize and clump with cold liquids, so the hot starchy water is essential to emulsify it. The finished sauce should be the consistency of heavy cream: fluid but coat the inside of the bowl. Taste it now — it should be intensely seasoned, almost too salty, too spicy, too nutty when tasted alone. It will come into balance once it coats a full portion of noodles. Adjust accordingly: more vinegar for brightness, more chili oil for heat, more sesame paste for richness, more cooking water to loosen.
4. Add the hot, drained noodles directly into the sauce bowl. Spoon a generous mound of the crispy pork topping onto the noodles without mixing it in yet. Scatter the scallion rings on top. At the table, instruct your diner to toss everything vigorously with chopsticks for 30 seconds before eating — the tossing distributes the sauce evenly, incorporates the pork into every strand, and emulsifies the last of the residual noodle starch into the sauce. This is the 'dan dan' motion the dish is named for: the bouncing, carrying-pole rhythm of street vendors who shook their portable noodle buckets to coat each serving. Each mouthful should deliver noodle, sauce, and pork in roughly equal amounts. Serve immediately — this dish waits for no one.
