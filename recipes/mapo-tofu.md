# Mapo Tofu

Adapted from "Mapo Tofu" by ForkRecipe Kitchen, from the ForkRecipe dataset.
This adaptation is licensed under [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/).

Silken tofu bobbing in a rust-red sea of doubanjiang, fermented black beans, and ground pork — fiery, tingling, deeply savoury, and almost dangerously moreish. The Sichuan peppercorn delivers its signature ma (numbing) alongside the la (heat) of the chilies.

Serves about 3 · 25 minutes · Contains: celery, gluten, soy, wheat

## Ingredients

- 1⅔ cups silken or medium-firm tofu (400 g)
- ⅔ cup ground pork, 20% fat (150 g)
- 2½ tablespoons doubanjiang, Pixian broad bean chili paste (40 g)
- 1½ tablespoons fermented black beans, roughly chopped; douchi
- 4 garlic cloves, minced
- A knob of fresh ginger, finely minced
- 1¼ teaspoons Sichuan peppercorns, toasted and ground
- 2 teaspoons dried chili flakes, Sichuan or Korean
- 1¼ cups chicken stock, or unsalted vegetable stock (300 ml)
- 1 tablespoon light soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon cornstarch, mixed with equal water; slurry
- 3 tablespoons neutral oil, vegetable or grapeseed (45 ml)
- 1 scallion, green parts only, thinly sliced (20 g)

## Method

1. Cut the tofu into 2cm cubes and place carefully in a bowl. Bring a pot of lightly salted water to a gentle boil. Slide the tofu cubes in and blanch for 2 minutes — do not stir aggressively, as silken tofu shatters easily. Drain with a spider or slotted spoon onto a plate lined with kitchen paper. This step firms the tofu surface slightly, seasons it from within, and removes any beany off-flavours — it is the difference between mapo tofu with clean, distinct cubes and a broken, murky mess.
2. Heat the oil in a wok or wide heavy pan over high heat until wisps of smoke appear. Add the ground pork and spread it into a thin layer. Let it sear undisturbed for 90 seconds until the bottom is golden and fat has rendered. Break it up and cook, stirring occasionally, for 3–4 more minutes until all pink is gone and the pork has rendered its fat and some pieces are golden and slightly crisp. Do not drain the fat — it carries the chili and fermented flavour into the sauce.
3. Add the doubanjiang to the wok and stir-fry with the pork for 2 minutes over high heat until the paste darkens from red to a deep rust-brown and the oil in the wok turns a vivid crimson — this is the hallmark of properly fried doubanjiang. Add the fermented black beans, garlic, and ginger; stir-fry for 1 more minute. Add the dried chili flakes. The wok should smell intensely of fermented chili, garlic, and caramelised pork — deeply savoury and slightly harsh in the raw stage, which will mellow once the stock goes in.
4. Pour the stock into the wok and scrape up any stuck bits from the bottom. Bring to a simmer, then gently slide in the blanched tofu cubes using a spatula. Add the soy sauce. Stir by gently rocking the wok and using a spatula to move the sauce — never stir through the tofu with a spoon or it will shatter. Simmer gently for 4–5 minutes so the tofu absorbs the sauce from the outside inward. The sauce will thin from the tofu's water — the cornstarch slurry in the next step will bring it back.
5. Give the cornstarch slurry a quick stir (it settles fast) and pour it around the edge of the wok in a slow, thin stream, gently rocking the wok as you add it. Cook for 30–60 seconds until the sauce thickens to a glossy, coating consistency that clings to the tofu. Remove from heat. Sprinkle the toasted, ground Sichuan peppercorn over the top — do not add it during cooking, or the numbing compounds will dissipate. Scatter the sliced scallion. Serve immediately over steamed rice. The ma (numbing) sensation builds over 30–60 seconds after eating.
