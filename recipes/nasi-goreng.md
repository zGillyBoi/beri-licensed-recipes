# Nasi Goreng

Adapted from "Nasi Goreng" by ForkRecipe Kitchen, from the ForkRecipe dataset.
This adaptation is licensed under [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/).

Indonesia's national fried rice — day-old rice stir-fried in a screaming-hot wok with shrimp paste, kecap manis, and sambal, scrambled with egg until each grain is coated in the dark, sweet-savory glaze and the wok edges smoke.

Serves about 4 · 25 minutes · Contains: crustaceans, egg, gluten, peanuts, soy, wheat

## Ingredients

- 3¼ cups day-old cooked jasmine rice, cold and separated (600 g)
- 2 eggs, large
- 1¾ teaspoons terasi, toasted; shrimp paste
- 2½ tablespoons Kecap manis, sweet soy sauce (40 ml)
- 1 tablespoon regular soy sauce or kecap asin
- 1 tablespoon sambal oelek or fresh red chili, sliced (20 g)
- 2 shallots, thinly sliced (60 g)
- 4 garlic cloves, minced (20 g)
- 2 tablespoons neutral oil, vegetable or peanut (30 ml)
- 30 g fried shallots, cucumber, spring onion, and/or a fried egg, to serve

## Method

1. Break up the cold day-old rice with your fingers or a fork until no clumps remain — every grain should be individual and dry. Cold, separated grains fry; fresh warm rice steams and turns to paste. If using solid terasi (shrimp paste), break off the required amount and toast it briefly: wrap in foil and hold over a low gas flame for 1 minute per side, or dry-fry in a small pan until fragrant and slightly crumbly. The toasting kills any fishy rawness and converts the paste into a powerfully savory, nutty compound. Crumble the toasted terasi into a small dish. Have all other ingredients measured and within arm's reach — nasi goreng moves fast.
2. Heat a wok or large cast-iron skillet over the highest flame you have until a drop of water vaporizes instantly on contact — this takes 2–3 minutes. Add the oil; it should ripple and shimmer immediately. Add the sliced shallots and stir-fry for 60 seconds until translucent and beginning to colour. Add the minced garlic and crumbled terasi, pressing the terasi against the hot wok with the spatula to break it up. Stir-fry for 30 seconds — the kitchen will fill with a powerful, savory, slightly funky aroma that quickly becomes complex and toasted. Add the sambal and toss for 15 seconds. The wok should be smoking lightly.
3. Push the aromatics to one side of the wok. Crack both eggs into the empty space. Leave them to set for 10–15 seconds without touching — the yolk should just begin to set at the edges. Then scramble them roughly with the spatula, breaking up into large, irregular curds. Before the egg is fully set and while it still looks slightly wet and shiny, push the aromatics back over the eggs and fold everything together. Partially-set egg integrates into the rice better than fully-cooked scrambled egg, which clumps separately.
4. Add the separated rice to the wok in two additions, tossing vigorously after each — use a spatula and a rapid scooping-and-flipping motion. Pour in the kecap manis and regular soy sauce over the rice (not the aromatics directly, which can burn) and toss continuously for 2–3 minutes. Every grain should be coated in the dark, mahogany-brown glaze. The rice may crackle and pop as individual grains toast against the wok surface — this is the wok hei you want. Taste and adjust with soy or kecap manis. Plate immediately.
5. Plate the nasi goreng immediately — it does not wait well. Top with a fried egg (sunny-side up, the yolk runny), crispy fried shallots scattered over the top, sliced cucumber alongside, and spring onion. The fried egg's yolk, broken at the table, acts as a sauce for the rice. The fried shallots add a final crunch and sweetness that the recipe sorely needs after all that heat. A few slices of cucumber cut through the richness. Serve with extra sambal on the side for those who want more heat.
